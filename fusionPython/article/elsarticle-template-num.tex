%% Abstract
\begin{abstract}
Proper orthogonal decomposition (POD) is often used as an interpretable front-end for sparse flow reconstruction and data fusion, but its reliability is uncertain when the wake is wall-confined, transient and regime-dependent. This study tests that reliability on a controlled two-dimensional computational data set of a circular cylinder in a finite rectangular tank. Reynolds number, wall proximity, spatial windowing, grid resolution and body translation are varied to diagnose full-field POD reconstruction error. A second benchmark then evaluates pressure reconstruction from eight synthetic wall-mounted pressure sensors using mean-field reconstruction, global and regime-local full-field POD projection, global gappy POD and regime-local gappy POD. The full-field results show that, over the sampled range $Re=10^3-10^4$, reconstruction error is much more sensitive to wall proximity than to Reynolds number. The most confined wakes also lose the quasi-periodicity exploited in canonical cylinder-wake POD analyses. In the pressure benchmark, global full-field POD reduces the average pressure nRMSE from 2.26 for the mean field to 1.97, while regime-local full-field POD reaches 2.05. Global wall-sensor gappy POD only reaches 2.23, and regime-local gappy POD becomes poorly conditioned, with a median condition number of $1.49\times10^4$ for the sensor-to-mode map. These results frame the data set as a diagnostic 2D benchmark rather than a high-Reynolds-number physical model, and show that energy retention, local bases and wall-sensor fit are insufficient indicators of robust sparse observability in confined wakes.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec1}
%% Labels are used to cross-reference an item using \ref command.

Sparse reconstruction of unsteady flow fields is a central problem in computational fluid dynamics when only a small number of experimental measurements are available. In applications ranging from wake estimation to bio-inspired hydrodynamic sensing, CFD can provide spatially complete but imperfect fields, whereas pressure or velocity sensors provide sparse but physically grounded information. Data-fusion and state-estimation strategies therefore seek to combine these sources into a coherent flow estimate. In addition to ensemble filtering approaches [De Marinis and Obrist, 2021], observer-based data assimilation has recently been used to couple time-averaged 3D-PIV measurements with RANS simulations while quantifying how forced-data density changes wake topology and reconstruction quality [Saredi et al., 2021].
Reduced-order modelling offers a complementary route for this task. A high-dimensional flow field is first projected onto a compact basis, after which sparse measurements can be mapped onto modal coefficients by regression, filtering or control-oriented estimation. Proper orthogonal decomposition (POD) remains one of the most common bases because it is energy-optimal for the snapshot ensemble and provides interpretable coherent structures [Berkooz et al., 1993; Chatterjee, 2000]. It is particularly successful in canonical cylinder wakes with well-established periodic vortex shedding [Noack et al., 2003; Tadmor et al., 2010]. Recent work in non-intrusive model reduction for vortex-induced vibrations further illustrates that POD remains a relevant building block for wake ROMs, while also emphasizing robustness with respect to model dimension and operating conditions [Gkimisis et al., 2024].
The present application is motivated by hydrodynamic sensing in confined experimental tanks. Fish perceive complex flows through the lateral line [Coombs et al., 1988], and recent experimental pipelines combine high-speed video, wall-mounted pressure sensors and simplified numerical simulations to infer the surrounding flow. The HAACHAMA pipeline, for example, extracts body kinematics from video recordings and generates two-dimensional CFD simulations in a tank-like geometry [Lagneaux et al., 2025]. In this context, freely swimming fish may accelerate, stop, turn or move close to the tank wall without following a repeatable trajectory. The biological system provides the motivation, but the computational question is broader: can a POD basis remain stable enough for sparse-flow data fusion when wakes interact strongly with nearby walls and when operating conditions change during a run?
This question is nontrivial because wall proximity changes cylinder-wake topology, suppresses or distorts vortex shedding, and induces regime transitions that are absent in unbounded configurations [Price et al., 2002; Chakraborty et al., 2004; Rao et al., 2013]. Such changes may not only alter the physical flow but also destabilize the low-dimensional representation used by a ROM. This is especially important for system-identification workflows such as POD-SINDy, where sparse regression assumes that the modal coefficients evolve on a smooth, low-dimensional manifold [Brunton et al., 2016]. If the POD basis changes abruptly with wall distance, downstream inference becomes fragile even if the retained energy remains high.
This paper does not propose a new reduced-order modelling algorithm. Its objective is to delimit the validity domain of a classical POD-based ROM front-end for data fusion in confined unsteady wakes. A synthetic but controlled 2D CFD data set of a circular cylinder in a finite rectangular domain is used to vary Reynolds number, wall proximity, spatial resolution, domain windowing and body translation. The resulting full-field reconstruction errors are then complemented by a sparse wall-pressure reconstruction benchmark. The contribution is therefore a diagnostic map of when classical POD and gappy POD provide, or fail to provide, a stable foundation for sparse-sensor data fusion in wall-bounded environments.

\section{Background}
\label{sec2}
%% Use \subsection commands to start a subsection.
\subsection{Data-fusion context}
\label{subsec2.1}

The motivating HAACHAMA workflow combines two information sources. The first consists of sparse but high-fidelity pressure measurements acquired from eight sensors mounted along the tank wall [Porcon et al., 2024]. The second is a low-fidelity digital twin generated with the LilyPad platform [Maertens and Weymouth, 2015], which produces pressure, velocity and vorticity fields on a two-dimensional grid. The intended fusion task is to retain the spatial completeness of CFD while correcting it with physically accurate measurements. For a \textit{Computers \& Fluids} audience, this task can be viewed as a computational state-estimation problem with sparse observations and imperfect simulations. The biological tank context is one instance of a broader class of wall-bounded, data-scarce wake-reconstruction problems.

\subsection{Proper orthogonal decomposition}
\label{subsec2.2}
POD constructs a low-dimensional linear representation of a flow field by decomposing a set of snapshots into orthogonal spatial modes ordered by decreasing energy content. Given a flow field $u(x,t)$ and a truncation $r$, POD approximates its evolution as $u(x,t) \approx \overline{u}(x) + \sum\nolimits_{i=1}^r a_i(t)\,\phi_i(x)$ where $\overline{u}(x)$ is the temporal mean, $\phi_i(x)$ are the spatial modes, and $a_i(t)$ are modal coefficients. When applied to canonical flows such as unbounded cylinder wakes, this decomposition yields compact representations dominated by a small number of coherent structures [Noack et al., 2003; Tadmor et al., 2010].

Though complex problems such as water-entry of a cylinder have been successfully modeled using POD [Sha et al., 2023], the effectiveness of this representation relies on assumptions of dynamical coherence and regime consistency [Loiseau et al., 2021], which may be violated under strong confinement or transient conditions.

\subsection{SINDy and ROM interpretability}
\label{subsec2.3}
The sparse identification of nonlinear dynamics (SINDy) framework identifies compact dynamical systems from time series by sparse regression on a candidate library [Brunton et al., 2016]. Applied to POD coefficients, SINDy requires a stable and sufficiently smooth reduced state. Variations in POD bases caused by wall effects, transients or changes of operating condition directly compromise the interpretability and predictive value of the identified equations. The SINDy discussion is therefore retained only as downstream motivation: the present paper tests whether the reduced coordinates supplied to such methods are stable enough to justify later inference.

\subsection{Wall-induced regimes and reduced representations}
\label{subsec2.4}
Studies of a circular cylinder near a wall have identified proximity-dependent regimes characterized by changes in vortex shedding, force coefficients and wake topology [Price et al., 2002; Chakraborty et al., 2004; Rao et al., 2013]. Price et al. [2002] separated the fixed-wall wake into very-small-gap, small-gap, intermediate-gap and large-gap regions: at the smallest gaps the gap flow is suppressed and regular vortex shedding is lost, at intermediate gaps vortex shedding begins to reappear, and at larger gaps the wall boundary-layer separation weakens. Later PIV measurements showed that decreasing gap ratio makes the wake increasingly asymmetric, promotes secondary vortices near the wall and produces irregular shedding below a critical gap [He et al., 2017]. These works clarify the physics of wall-confined wakes, but they do not directly answer whether regime transitions translate into smooth variations in a POD basis or into representation instabilities. The present work addresses this representational question and then tests whether wall-pressure observations can recover the associated full pressure fields through gappy POD.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec3}
\subsection{Synthetic data set: cylinder in a finite rectangular tank}
\label{subsec3.1}
The final target system in this study, a fish, exhibits highly dynamic behavior, involving continuous shape deformation along smooth, organic trajectories. As a result, both swimming speed and proximity to the wall vary over time. However, for the purposes of the present investigation, a simplified CFD dataset was employed in which these complexities were excluded. The Reynolds number, representing speed (under the assumption that all other parameters are constant in $Re=U\cdot L/\nu$), and wall proximity were held fixed for each simulation. No body deformation was introduced, and the geometry was simplified to a rigid cylinder.

Numerically, the simulations were conducted in a two-dimensional tank with dimensions of 2 meters in length and 1 meter in width, with incoming flow imposed from the left boundary. These dimensions were chosen to facilitate reproducibility and to ensure compatibility with potential future experimental validation. The cylinder diameter was fixed at 0.05 meters, consistent with the characteristic scale of small aquatic animals, while maintaining a ratio of at least 10:1 between tank half-width and cylinder diameter. This configuration approximated an effectively unbounded far side while retaining a nearby wall for the confinement study.

The cylinder was positioned at two-sevenths of the tank length from the left boundary, allowing sufficient upstream distance for the establishment of a steady incoming flow while preserving most of the domain for the development of a von Karman vortex street. Each simulation was run for a duration of 500 time units, with time steps defined by the relation (all quantities being expressed in non-dimensional form using the grid size and the uniform flow velocity as reference scale):
\begin{align}
    \Delta t= \text{min}(\frac{1}{\text{max}_{i,j}(|u_x(i,j)|+|u_y(i,j)|) + 3\cdot\nu},1)
\end{align}
with $u_x(i,j)$ the horizontal velocity at grid point (i, j); $u_y(i,j)$ the vertical velocity at grid point (i, j); $\nu$ the kinematic viscosity.

The Reynolds number was varied across a range from $10^3$ to $10^4$, with simulation scenarios uniformly distributed within this interval. This range corresponds to realistic swimming speeds for fish while remaining feasible for replication in towing tank experiments. Similarly, the wall proximity, defined as the distance between the cylinder surface and the nearest tank wall and normalized by the cylinder diameter, was varied from $0.44D$ to $4.19D$ in the main stationary matrix.

To investigate the impact of computational parameters (\hyperref[fig7]{figure~1}), the simulation window was progressively reduced in size by factors of 2 and 4 in both horizontal and vertical directions, effectively zooming in on the cylinder. This windowing strategy allowed for the evaluation of how restricting the field of interest to the immediate vicinity of the swimmer, while excluding distant boundaries or obstacles, affects POD performance. Narrowing the window is also relevant for realistic cases where a fish swims along organic trajectories: a sliding window centered on the animal changes the reference frame and moves the analysis closer to a local wake problem. Windowing was applied either a priori, by computing POD only on the reduced domain, or a posteriori, by applying POD to the full simulation and reconstructing the windowed region.

Additionally, the resolution of the CFD simulations was doubled for a subset of the dataset to examine the sensitivity of POD behavior to spatial discretization.

To further approach realistic conditions, a portion of the dataset was modified to include translational motion. In these cases, the cylinder was not stationary but traversed a 1-meter straight path from right to left through initially still fluid. Reynolds number and wall proximity were maintained as in the stationary cases, implying that simulation duration varied inversely with speed. To allow comparison with the default fixed-cylinder simulations, matched time-cropped versions of the stationary cases were also generated.

Reynolds number and wall proximity were varied independently throughout the dataset to isolate their respective effects on POD-based representations. The high-Reynolds-number cases are used as a controlled two-dimensional numerical benchmark of representation robustness; they are not interpreted as quantitatively faithful models of physical high-Re cylinder wakes, which are known to become three-dimensional at much lower Reynolds numbers [Williamson, 1996].

\subsection{POD Computation}
\label{subsec3.2}
For each simulation time step, the pressure or vorticity fields were flattened into column vectors and assembled into a snapshot matrix. Prior to decomposition, the temporal mean was subtracted from each snapshot to ensure zero-centered data. POD was computed via singular value decomposition (SVD) [Harris et al., 2020].

The number of retained modes corresponded to 99\% of the cumulative energy content. Reconstruction errors were quantified as the mean root-mean-square error (RMSE) over the entire simulation duration.

To provide a reference for evaluation, results were compared against the canonical case of a fixed cylinder at Reynolds number $Re=10^2$ in an unbounded domain, as originally analyzed by Noack et al. [2003].

\subsection{Sparse wall-pressure reconstruction}
\label{subsec3.3}
The sparse benchmark was performed on the stationary pressure-map matrix in Table~\ref{tab:sparse_dataset}. For each case, snapshots were split chronologically into 60\% training, 20\% validation and 20\% test data. The observation operator $H$ selected eight uniformly spaced pressure samples along the nearest wall, excluding the two corner cells, to mimic the wall-mounted sensor configuration used in the experimental workflow.

Given a pressure snapshot $p(t)$, a POD basis approximates
\begin{align}
p(t) \approx \overline{p}+\Phi_r a(t),
\end{align}
where $\overline{p}$ is the training mean, $\Phi_r$ contains the first $r$ modes and $a(t)$ are modal coefficients. In full-field POD projection, the coefficients were computed from the full pressure field. In wall-sensor gappy POD, only the sparse observations $y(t)=Hp(t)$ were available, and the modal coefficients were estimated through ridge-regularized least squares,
\begin{align}
\hat{a}(t)=
\arg\min_a \left\|H\Phi_r a-\left(y(t)-H\overline{p}\right)\right\|_2^2
\,+\,\lambda \|a\|_2^2 .
\end{align}
Five pressure reconstructions were compared: the training mean field, global full-field POD projection, regime-local full-field POD projection, global wall-sensor gappy POD, and regime-local wall-sensor gappy POD. The full-field projections are oracle diagnostics because they use the complete test pressure field to estimate modal coefficients; they measure basis representation quality before sparse observability is imposed. The global basis was trained across all stationary pressure cases. The regime-local methods used separate bases for the small-, intermediate- and large-gap regimes of Table~\ref{tab:sparse_dataset}, while retaining the same eight wall sensors for the gappy variant. POD ranks and ridge factors were selected on the validation snapshots; full-field POD was allowed up to 48 modes, whereas gappy POD was capped at eight modes to avoid using more modal coefficients than sensors.

Sparse reconstruction accuracy was measured on held-out test snapshots with the normalized Frobenius error
\begin{align}
\mathrm{nRMSE} =
\frac{\|P_{\mathrm{test}}-\widehat{P}_{\mathrm{test}}\|_F}
{\|P_{\mathrm{test}}-\langle P_{\mathrm{test}}\rangle_t\|_F}.
\end{align}
Values above one indicate errors larger than the intrinsic unsteady pressure fluctuations of the test case. The sensor residual and the condition number of $H\Phi_r$ were also recorded to separate full-field accuracy from sparse observability.

\subsection{Periodicity Analysis}
\label{subsec3.4}
In Noack's reference study, the analysis was based on the assumption of a periodic regime. POD was applied exclusively to the established periodic segment of the flow, and the resulting modal basis was subsequently extended with a ``shift mode'' to capture the distortion between the linearly unstable base flow and the marginally stable mean flow.

To assess the validity of this assumption under more realistic conditions, a periodicity analysis was performed on the most challenging case in the dataset defined as the scenario with the highest Reynolds number and the smallest wall proximity. Each simulation snapshot was flattened into a one-dimensional vector and cross-correlated with the final snapshot of the simulation.

In a fully periodic system, peaks in correlation would be expected at intervals corresponding to vortex shedding cycles. Conversely, the absence of recurring peaks would indicate nonperiodicity and a breakdown of the cyclic behavior assumed in classical POD applications.

\subsection{Tests Performed}
\label{subsec3.5}
Four controlled test protocols were designed to assess the robustness of POD under varying physical and computational conditions.

In the first series of tests, the distance between the center of the cylinder and the wall was held constant while the Reynolds number was incrementally varied to span the full range of values. This procedure was repeated for each discrete wall proximity value in the dataset.

The protocol was then reversed: the Reynolds number was fixed, and wall proximity was varied across its defined range.

These two stages of varying Reynolds number at fixed proximity, and varying proximity at fixed Reynolds number were subsequently applied in the three other tests corresponding to the previously mentioned subsections of the dataset: (i) simulations with spatial windowing, (ii) simulations with doubled spatial resolution, and (iii) translating-cylinder scenarios. Sparse wall-pressure reconstruction was evaluated only on the stationary pressure maps, over the full $Re \times h$ matrix, so that the added benchmark remained directly tied to the experimental wall-sensor layout.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Lack of Periodicity in Wall-confined Wakes}
Correlation analysis of the worst-case scenario, defined by $Re=10^4$ and minimal wall gap, revealed no consistent recurrence of correlation peaks over time (\hyperref[fig1]{figure~2}). This absence of a dominant periodic structure indicated that a stable vortex shedding regime did not emerge under these conditions.

\begin{figure}[]
\centering{\includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{figures/figure2_autocorrelation.eps}}
\caption{Correlation between each frame and the last one, in the worst-case scenario (highest speed with a Reynolds number of $10^4$ and smallest distance $h$ of half a diameter of the cylinder).}
\label{fig1}
\end{figure}

This result undermines a foundational assumption commonly made in classical POD applications to cylinder wakes, where the decomposition is performed on a quasi-periodic segment of the flow, and supplemented a posteriori with additional modes to capture transient behavior. In contrast, the findings here suggest that such an approach is not generally valid in wall-confined or highly transient flow regimes.

As a result, POD was applied to the entire duration of all simulations throughout this study, ensuring a consistent protocol regardless of periodicity. For comparison with the canonical case, the same full-simulation POD procedure was also applied to the reference scenario.

\subsection{Sensitivity to Reynolds Number}
\label{subsec4.2}
For Reynolds numbers $Re \geq 2000$, reconstruction error exhibited only weak dependence on Reynolds number, as shown in \hyperref[fig2]{figure~3}. This finding indicates that spontaneous changes in swimming speed, such as acceleration or deceleration, would not, in isolation, cause POD-based reconstruction errors to be impossible to track over the course of an experiment. For Reynolds numbers below 2000, reconstruction error exhibited a strong dependence on Re, yet since this regime lies outside the range relevant to this study, it was not analyzed further.

\begin{figure}[]
\centering{\includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{figures/figure3_rmse_vs_re.eps}}
\caption{Evolution of the mean RMSE of reconstruction with 99\% of the initial kinetic energy with respect to the Reynolds number for the 10 available distances $h$. The reference corresponds to the unbounded, stationary cylinder wake at Re = $10^2$ analyzed by Noack et al. [2003], in periodic regime.}
\label{fig2}
\end{figure}

This result provides an encouraging indication of the robustness of POD in dynamically evolving conditions typical of fish locomotion.

Additionally, the same figure reveals a more pronounced sensitivity of reconstruction error to wall proximity. This observation motivates the subsequent analysis, which is aimed at characterizing the nature of this dependency in detail.

\subsection{Strong Nonlinear Dependence on Wall Proximity}
\label{subsec4.3}
Wall proximity emerged as a dominant factor influencing POD reconstruction performance. Reconstruction RMSE increased by up to a factor of four in response to relatively small changes in the gap between the cylinder and the wall.

Three distinct flow regimes were identified (\hyperref[fig3]{figure~4}), consistent with the wall-gap mechanisms reported in the literature but not intended as exact replications of those threshold values. The relevant physical picture is that the wall first suppresses or strongly distorts the gap-side shear layer, then allows intermittent or asymmetric shedding as the gap opens, and finally approaches a weakly confined wake at larger gaps [Price et al., 2002; He et al., 2017]:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Small gap regime ($G/D \leq 0.44$): strong wall interaction, inhibited gap-side circulation and loss of regular alternate shedding.
    \item Intermediate gap regime ($0.44 < G/D \leq 1.68$): circulation exists on both sides of the cylinder, but the wake remains asymmetric and sensitive to wall-induced secondary structures.
    \item Large gap regime ($G/D > 1.68$): the wake approaches the unbounded-cylinder organization, although residual wall effects remain in the finite tank.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[]
\centering{\includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{figures/figure1_rmse_vs_gap.eps}}
\caption{Evolution of the mean RMSE of reconstruction with 99\% of the initial kinetic energy with respect to the distance between the center of the cylinder and the wall divided by the diameter of the cylinder for the 10 available Reynolds number $Re$ and the reference scenario.}
\label{fig3}
\end{figure}

Although the number of proximity values sampled in certain regimes, particularly the small-gap regime, was limited, the trend was sufficiently pronounced to confirm a strong and nonlinear sensitivity of POD reconstruction to wall proximity.

This sensitivity is not inherently problematic in controlled scenarios where position is known precisely or motion is constrained to near-linear trajectories. However, in the broader context of hydrodynamic sensing in freely swimming fish, it poses a significant challenge. This is due to two compounding factors: (i) fish frequently operate within the small- and intermediate-gap regimes, and (ii) wall proximity cannot be measured with sufficient precision to prevent high uncertainty in reconstruction quality. As a result, small errors in estimating the fish's distance to the wall can lead to unpredictable and potentially chaotic variations in reconstruction accuracy, thereby compromising data fusion reliability.

\subsection{Windowing and Resolution Effects}
\label{subsec4.4}
Spatial windowing was found to consistently degrade POD reconstruction accuracy, as illustrated in \hyperref[fig4]{figure~5}. This effect was observed regardless of whether windowing was applied a priori (before POD computation) or a posteriori (after global decomposition). These findings indicate that spatial cropping is not a viable strategy for mitigating the increase in reconstruction error induced by wall proximity.

\begin{figure}[]
\centering{\includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{figures/zoom.eps}}
\caption {Evolution of the mean RMSE of reconstruction with 99\% of the initial kinetic energy with respect to the Reynolds number averaged over the 10 available distances, for four windowing scenarios.}
\label{fig4}
\end{figure}

Furthermore, windowing does not enable a viable sliding-window rebase strategy to emulate non-moving scenarios in the presence of body motion. As such, it fails to compensate for reconstruction errors which could potentially be arising from translational dynamics or wall-induced distortion.

Doubling the spatial resolution of the simulation led to modest improvements in reconstruction accuracy, reducing RMSE by less than a factor of two (\hyperref[fig5]{figure~6}). However, this improvement came at the cost of approximately four times the computational load. As a result, the trade-off between computational efficiency and reconstruction fidelity does not favor the use of higher resolution in this context.

\begin{figure}[]
\centering{\includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{figures/resolution.eps}}
\caption {Evolution of the mean RMSE of reconstruction with 99\% of the initial kinetic energy with respect to the distance between the center of the cylinder and the wall divided by the diameter of the cylinder averaged on the 10 available Reynolds number for two resolutions.}
\label{fig5}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Translating Cylinder}
\label{subsec4.5}
When simulation durations were matched by cropping the default cases to the same time span, translating-cylinder scenarios produced reconstruction errors approximately 33\% higher than their fixed-cylinder counterparts (\hyperref[fig6]{figure~7}). This confirmed that body translation introduces additional complexity that impairs POD reconstruction accuracy.

\begin{figure}[]
\centering{\includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/translation_side_by_side.eps}}
\caption {Evolution of the mean RMSE of reconstruction with 99$\%$ of the initial kinetic energy with respect to the distance between the center of the cylinder and the wall divided by the diameter of the cylinder for the 10 available Reynolds number $Re$ and the reference scenario for a moving cylinder in an initially still water (left side) against default setup with a similarly cropped duration (right side).}
\label{fig6}
\end{figure}

However, the increase in error appeared primarily as an offset rather than a change in trend. The overall dependency of reconstruction error on wall proximity remained consistent between translating and stationary configurations. This suggests that, although POD reconstruction degrades under body motion, it does not exhibit a strong sensitivity to translational effects per se.

This comparison between fixed and translating cases also highlighted the specific impact of transients. While the motion itself exerted limited influence, scenarios focusing on the early, non-periodic phase of the flow (despite the absence of a clear pseudo-steady regime) showed a substantial reduction in reconstruction error. In fact, reconstruction RMSE values in these transient segments were an order of magnitude lower than those in the full-duration stationary cases. For wall distances beyond 2.94 cylinder diameters, reconstruction errors were even lower than those reported in the canonical unbounded scenario analyzed by Noack, suggesting that much of the error in classical setups originates from reconstructions over the "steady-state" regime.

The focus on transient phases also revealed different flow regime characteristics relative to wall proximity. In these cases, the small-gap and intermediate regimes appeared to blend more smoothly. Moreover, a regime shift was observed: in translating scenarios, the threshold between intermediate and large-gap regimes shifted toward larger distances, occurring at approximately $1.27$ cylinder diameters.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec5}
\subsection{Implication for POD-based data fusion}
\label{subsec5.1}
Although the sensitivity of wake dynamics to wall proximity is well documented, its consequences for POD-based data fusion are less frequently addressed. In hydrodynamic sensing applications, this sensitivity poses a critical challenge: the quality of a POD-based reduced-order representation becomes strongly dependent on wall distance, a parameter that is difficult to estimate accurately in experiments.

For freely swimming fish, wall proximity can vary continuously during a trial and may only be coarsely inferred from video data. Under such conditions, small uncertainties in position translate into large and difficult-to-predict variations in reconstruction error, undermining the stability required for downstream tasks such as system identification or sensor fusion.

The breakdown of periodicity in confined, high-Reynolds-number wakes further compromises classical POD workflows. Unlike canonical cylinder wakes, most confined flows do not consistently exhibit quasi-periodic regimes, forcing POD to operate on strongly transient data and preventing its restricted application or shift mode supplementation, as in the canonical example of Noack et al. [2003]. As a result, POD does not provide a sufficiently stable foundation for interpretable data fusion pipelines such as POD-SINDy in the present context.

The sparse pressure benchmark adds a second constraint. Even when a POD basis represents the pressure field better than the mean field, the associated coefficients may not be observable from wall pressure alone. The regime-local full-field POD diagnostic shows that localizing by the present gap regimes is not automatically beneficial for representation quality; the global pressure basis remained stronger. The global gappy POD basis then had acceptable conditioning but recovered only a small fraction of the full-field POD improvement. Conversely, regime-local gappy bases were more tailored to the flow regime but became highly ill-conditioned from the same eight wall sensors. This trade-off suggests that basis adaptation and sensor placement cannot be treated independently in wall-bounded sparse reconstruction.

\subsection{Limitations of the present study}
\label{subsec5.2}
The present study deliberately adopts a simplified configuration in order to isolate the impact of confinement and transience on POD robustness. All simulations are two-dimensional and involve rigid cylindrical geometries, neglecting three-dimensional effects and body deformation that are present in real fish locomotion. This is a significant physical limitation at $Re=10^3-10^4$, since real circular-cylinder wakes transition to three-dimensional dynamics at much lower Reynolds numbers [Williamson, 1996]. The results should therefore be read as a controlled 2D diagnostic benchmark for reduced representations and sparse observability, not as quantitative predictions of high-Reynolds-number cylinder physics.

In addition, the parameter space is sampled with the objective of detecting sensitivity rather than exhaustively characterizing regime boundaries. The identification of flow regimes with respect to wall proximity is therefore qualitative and intended to support interpretation rather than to provide precise classification thresholds.

Finally, the analysis focuses on POD as a front-end for data fusion and system identification, rather than on optimizing sensor placement or proposing a new assimilation algorithm. More advanced placement strategies, adaptive dictionaries or nonlinear estimators may improve reconstruction quality in specific scenarios. The present negative result is narrower: a straightforward energy-based POD front-end, even with a regime-local variant, is not a robust sparse-reconstruction foundation for the wall-sensor configuration tested here.


\subsection{Outlook: alternative modeling strategies}
\label{subsec5.3}
The limitations identified in this case study motivate the exploration of alternative modeling approaches capable of accommodating strong nonlinearity, regime transitions, and heterogeneous boundary effects. In particular, data-driven methods based on nonlinear latent representations offer a promising direction.

Autoencoder-based architectures, including variational autoencoders, provide a means of learning low-dimensional latent spaces that capture shared features across heterogeneous data sources. Such representations may better capture the continuous deformation of flow structures induced by wall effects and body motion by enabling fusion of low-fidelity CFD-derived fields and sparse and noisy sensor data. More recently, diffusion-based generative models have demonstrated strong performance in scientific modeling. GenCast, introduced by Price et al. [2025], outperforms traditional numerical models in complex meteorological tasks while preserving power spectra, which is an essential requirement for physical fidelity. The relevance of diffusion models to flow field fusion has also been supported by recent contributions such as Huang et al. [2024] and Qiu et al. [2024], all of which point to the adaptability of these architectures for combining multi-fidelity or multimodal scientific data.

It would thus be of significant interest to apply those modern techniques to the reconstruction and analysis of flow fields generated by freely swimming fish, but a major limitation lies in the volume of training data required. Addressing this requirement will likely necessitate coordinated efforts to construct larger, standardized datasets combining experimental measurements and numerical simulations across a wide range of confined flow scenarios. Such datasets would not only support the development of more expressive models, but also enable systematic evaluation of their robustness under realistic experimental constraints.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec6}
This case study examined the robustness of POD in wall-confined, unsteady flows representative of experimental tank conditions in biological hydrodynamics. Four main findings emerge.

First, beyond moderate values, Reynolds number does not constitute the dominant source of instability in POD-based reconstructions. For Reynolds numbers in the range $\mathcal{O}(10^3-10^4)$, reconstruction error shows only weak sensitivity to changes in flow speed, suggesting that variations in swimming speed alone are unlikely to cause severe degradation of POD-based representations in this regime.

Second, wall proximity is identified as the primary driver of reconstruction instability. POD reconstruction error exhibits a strong and nonlinear dependence on the distance to the nearest solid boundary, with small changes in proximity producing large variations in reconstruction quality. The emergence of distinct proximity-dependent regimes, consistent with established hydrodynamic classifications, indicates that this behavior reflects genuine physical transitions rather than numerical artifacts.

Third, mitigation strategies such as spatial windowing, increased spatial resolution, or accounting for body translation do not fundamentally resolve this instability. While increased resolution yields modest improvements and translation affects absolute error levels, neither approach mitigates the dominant influence of wall proximity.

Fourth, sparse wall-pressure reconstruction is limited by observability. Global full-field POD projection improves pressure reconstruction, while the tested regime-local full-field POD remains slightly worse. Gappy POD from eight wall sensors recovers only a small part of the global full-field improvement. Regime-local gappy bases do not resolve the issue and can become poorly conditioned with respect to the fixed wall-sensor layout.

These conclusions are specific to the confined, transient and two-dimensional conditions examined in this case study. POD remains effective in canonical configurations with weak boundary effects and stationary regimes, but its underlying assumptions are poorly matched to strongly confined experimental environments. By delineating these limits of applicability, this case study highlights the need for alternative modeling approaches capable of supporting robust data fusion in heterogeneous, data-scarce and dynamically evolving flow conditions. Ultimately, advancing hydrodynamic data fusion in biologically realistic conditions will depend on modeling tools that balance physical interpretability with expressive capacity while explicitly accounting for sparse observability.
