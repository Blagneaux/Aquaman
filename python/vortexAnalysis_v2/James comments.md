Across most simulation variants and confidence thresholds, measured pressure segments were classified more frequently as fish-like than as cylinder-like (Figure 4). Because both simulated bodies followed the same observed centre-of-mass trajectories, this pattern suggests that trajectory alone was insufficient to account for the measured pressure signatures. Representing the fish's body profile and deformation generally produced pressure patterns more consistent with the experimental recordings.

The clearest preference for the fish-shaped model occured in the X-deformation variant, which retained the original measure contours and included boundary motion along both tank axes. With 95% of simulated pressure variance retained and a confidence threshold of 0.99, 132 of 436 measured segments were classified as fish-like (30.3%; 95% Wilson binomial CI: 26.2-34.7% \[30]), compared with 19 classified as cylinder-like (4.4%; 95% CI: 2.8-6.7%). The remaining 285 segments (65.4%) were undetermined. Although increasing the confidence threshold reduced the proportion of segments assigned to either model, the difference between the fish-like and cylinder-like proportions remained approximately constant. Thus, the relative preference for the fish-shaped model persisted under increasingly stringent classification criteria, even though most segments could not be assigned confidently at the highest threshold. This result supports including local body motion along both tank axes when modeling the pressure signatures of freely swimming fish.

Constraining projected body size did not produce a corresponding improvement. Fitting the first-frame body profile along the reconstructed spine reduced the separation between simulated fish-shaped and cylinder pressure signals, and the experimental classifications became more sensitive to the confidence threshold. Combining this size constraint with two-component boundary forcing also failed to improve agreement relative to the X-deformation variant. Retaining frame-to-frame variation in the measured contours therefore provided a more useful representation for this comparison, although these results do not establish whether that variation arose from genuine changes in posture, out-of-plane motion or segmentation error.

The main pattern was also robust to the amount of simulated pressure variance retained during dimensionality reduction. Repeating the analysis with bases retaining 75% and 99% of simulated variance produced the same qualitative results (Supplementary Figures 3 and 4), indicating that the preference for the fish-shaped model was not specific to the 95% representation.

The classifiers distinguished overlapping model classes and provided a relative measure of consistency for variants that performed poorly on simulated test data. Their confidence scores therfore do not give the probability that either model correctly represents an experimental wake.
