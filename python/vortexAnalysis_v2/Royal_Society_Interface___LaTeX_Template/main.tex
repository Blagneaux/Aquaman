\title{Fine-scale locomotor actuation leaves detectable pressure signatures in fish wakes}

\author{Bastien Lagneaux$^{1}$, Corentin Porcon$^{1}$, Michael Mabelle$^{2}$, Paul Lagneaux$^{3}$, Thomas Omarini$^{1}$, Boai Sun$^{1}$, Nadia Maria Hamilton$^{4}$, Florence Razan$^{1}$, James Herbert-Read$^{4}$, Gurvan Jodin$^{1}$}

\begin{abstract}
Swimming fish generate hydrodynamic signatures that may be detected by other animals, but it remains unclear which details of body motion must be represented to reproduce those signatures. To test this, pressure measurements from three wall-mounted sensors were combined with two-dimensional numerical wake models driven by observed trajectories of striped eel catfish, \textit{Plotosus lineatus}. A deforming fish-shaped digital twin was compared with a cylinder following the same measured centre-of-mass paths. Measured pressure segments were more consistent with the deforming fish model than with the cylinder, especially when boundary motion along both tank axes was communicated to the flow solver. With $95\%$ of simulated pressure variance retained and a $0.99$ classification-confidence threshold, this model classified $30.3\%$ of measured segments as fish-like and $4.4\%$ as cylinder-like. Analysis of individual vortices indicated that displacement, spatial position, path geometry and persistence were more informative than raw vortex intensity alone. These results do not demonstrate behavioural use of wake cues, but they show that retaining fine-scale body deformation improves preservation of pressure signatures measured from swimming fish.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Swimming animals generate hydrodynamic wakes composed of vortices, velocity fluctuations and pressure variations. The structure of these wakes can reflect properties of the generating motion, body size, trajectory and recent movement history \cite{weihs1975}. Fish can detect local hydrodynamic disturbances through the lateral-line system, which responds to pressure gradients and water motion associated with nearby flow structures \cite{drucker2000,videler2002,coombs1988}. Fish can also exploit vortices in collective and wake-following contexts \cite{liao2003}. Hydrodynamic wakes are therefore potentially information-rich sensory cues, although the contribution of different components of body motion to those cues remains difficult to isolate in freely swimming animals.

A related body of work treats wake sensing as an inverse problem. Rather than predicting the flow generated by a known body, properties of the source are inferred from hydrodynamic measurements. Neural networks have classified canonical wakes behind oscillating foils from local vorticity measurements \cite{colvert2018}, and artificial neural models have identified idealised obstacle shapes from distant pressure or velocity fields in potential flow \cite{lakkam2019}. Artificial lateral-line systems have also combined body-surface pressure with neural networks to distinguish the shape and dimensions of moving underwater targets in simulations and experiments \cite{luo2024}. These studies show that local measurements can contain recoverable source-related information, but they rely on canonical, idealised or prescribed target flows. Whether sparse pressure measurements can distinguish signals associated with a freely swimming biological source from those of a simplified physical surrogate has received less attention.

Experimental access to the full wake is usually limited because flow measurements around living animals can perturb the animal or cover only part of the domain. A common strategy is therefore to replace the swimmer with a simplified body, such as a cylinder, flexible foil, finite-dipole swimmer or few-link articulated swimmer. These surrogates isolate elementary wake mechanisms and make simulation or experimental control tractable, but they simplify different aspects of the animal. In collective-motion and navigation models, finite-dipole models deliberately abstract both morphology and kinematics \cite{gazzola2015}, while reinforcement-learning studies may reduce the body to a three-link fish in potential flow, with deformation represented by two hinge angles \cite{jiao2021}. Higher-fidelity reinforcement-learning simulations can resolve vortical interactions around self-propelled swimmers, but still focus mainly on energetic optimization in controlled leader-follower steady swimming settings \cite{verma2018}. Recent robotic-fish studies have added richer control and sensing, including LSTM-based navigation through a cylinder-generated Karman vortex street \cite{feng2024} and pressure-informed, transformer-based control of a serially connected rigid swimmer in a free stream \cite{cui2024}. These approaches increase hydrodynamic and control realism, but their body motion and deformation remain more constrained than measured living-fish deformation.

More anatomically detailed simulations reduce geometric simplification by including realistic fish form and kinematics. Comparisons that exchanged carangiform and anguilliform forms and kinematics among self-propelled virtual swimmers showed distinct effects of form and motion on speed, efficiency and small-scale wake structure \cite{borazjani2010}. More recent wall-resolved simulations of lamprey and mackerel found swimmer-specific pressure and vorticity scaling, illustrating how body form and swimming mode jointly shape the surrounding flow \cite{daghooghi2025}. Jack-fish simulations showed that real kinematics can change hydrodynamic performance relative to prescribed motions, while still simplifying the trajectory to steady swimming and controlled wavelength variation \cite{khalid2021}. Flow-physics-informed models of carangiform schools use wake information from high-fidelity simulations to predict beneficial relative positions, but likewise assume steady swimming and prescribed kinematic or positional parameters \cite{zhou2025}. Fish-like NACA profiles and robotic platforms provide strong control over gait, tailbeat phase or body-wave prescription \cite{maertens2016,li2021}. Across these approaches, increasing geometric detail does not necessarily preserve the unconstrained motion of a living swimmer.

Together, these literatures leave two connected questions. The first is whether sparse pressure signals contain enough source-related information to separate a deforming fish-shaped model from a simple physical surrogate. Establishing that separation is necessary before the relative importance of trajectory, body profile and deformation can be evaluated. The second question is which parts of a living fish's measured motion must be represented to preserve pressure and vortex signatures. A rigid surrogate may reproduce the centre-of-mass path or broad wake periodicity while omitting local surface motion and projected shape changes. Conversely, additional digital-twin detail is useful only if it improves agreement with measurements.

Here, a deforming fish-shaped digital twin and a cylinder driven by identical experimentally observed centre-of-mass paths were compared. Pressure measurements from three wall-mounted sensors provided a sparse, non-invasive reference. This made it possible to test whether measured pressure segments were more consistent with the fish-shaped model than with the trajectory-matched cylinder, and whether contour-size regularisation and inclusion of boundary motion along both tank axes changed that consistency. A classifier trained only on simulated fish and cylinder pressure segments provided a comparative metric. Throughout, ``fish-like'' means more consistent with the deforming digital twin in the learned pressure-signal space, but it does not imply direct reconstruction of the experimental wake or behavioural recognition by another fish.

\section{Materials and methods}

\subsection{Experimental data and sample accounting}

The data originated from infrared video recordings of 40 striped eel catfish, \textit{Plotosus lineatus}, each recorded in one video while swimming in a water tank equipped with three wall-mounted pressure sensors. Recordings were acquired at 100 frames s$^{-1}$ with an image resolution of 2000 $\times$ 1200 pixels. Fish body contours and sensor locations were visible in the same view, allowing fish motion to be related to pressure measured at the wall.

\begin{figure}[b]
    \centering{\includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/haachama.pdf}}
    \caption{Overview of the HAACHAMA pipeline used to generate a deforming fish-shaped digital twin and a cylinder surrogate. Infrared video provided the fish trajectory and body contour, while three wall-mounted sensors recorded experimental pressure. The fish-shaped digital twin and cylinder were driven by the same centre-of-mass trajectory. The pink centre-of-mass marker is a conceptual annotation added to help relate the experimental and simulated representations. It is not an output generated by the flow simulation or a direct result of the segmentation.}
    \label{fig:haachama}
\end{figure}

The 40 videos were segmented into 493 discrete samples in which a fish was visible. Fish contours were extracted with the HAACHAMA pipeline \cite{lagneaux2024,lagneaux2025}, summarised in Figure \ref{fig:haachama}. Of the 493 segmented samples, 403 (81.7\%) were retained: 367 samples processed without manual intervention, 17 requiring heavy temporal cropping, six with some poor segmentation and 13 containing a temporary circular-segmentation error. The remaining 90 samples were not retained. This filtering was completed before simulation and classifier analyses. Full sample accounting and exclusion categories are reported in supplementary table S1.

\subsection{Digital twins and simulation variants}

Each retained video frame was represented by a closed fish contour, and each sequence described both displacement and body deformation through time. The contours generated a deforming fish-shaped digital twin in LilyPad \cite{maertens2015}. The same measured centre-of-mass trajectory drove a cylindrical surrogate whose diameter was set from fish body width. The two bodies therefore followed the same observed path but differed in geometry and deformation.

The simulation coordinate system was fixed to the tank: $x$ was the long tank axis and $y$ was the transverse tank axis. Because fish swam predominantly along $x$, $x$ motion was approximately streamwise and $y$ motion captured most lateral undulation, but these were laboratory-coordinate components rather than instantaneous fish-centred axes.

Two uncertainties motivated the simulation variants. First, the independently segmented contours fluctuated in projected size between frames. These changes could represent segmentation noise, natural posture variation or out-of-plane motion such as body roll. A fixed profile based on the first frame was therefore tested to determine whether regularising projected size improved agreement with measured pressure. Second, the original implementation treated swimming as progression mainly along $x$ with deformation mainly along $y$, following a common approximation for idealised undulatory swimming. Real trajectories were not perfectly aligned with $x$, and local body motion could also contain an $x$ component when the fish turned or when the tail tip moved towards or away from the head. Two-component boundary forcing was tested to determine whether these secondary in-plane motions contributed to the pressure signature. The two tests produced four variants:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textit{Raw}: the original image-derived contours with the original $y$-only boundary forcing.
    \item \textit{Size-constrained}: a profile based on the first frame was fitted along the reconstructed spine in every frame. This preserved bending while constraining projected size, and the original $y$-only forcing was retained.
    \item \textit{X-deformation}: the original contours were retained, but both $x$ and $y$ boundary-motion components forced the fluid.
    \item \textit{Full}: the size constraint and two-component boundary forcing were combined.
\end{enumerate}
For the fixed cylinder geometry, the size constraint had no effect. Consequently, the raw and size-constrained cylinder simulations were identical, as were the x-deformation and full cylinder simulations. Further implementation details are provided in supplementary sections S6 and S7.

\subsection{Simulated vortices and pressure segments}

The experiment provided pressure at three wall locations rather than a spatially resolved wake field. Corresponding simulated pressure values were extracted at those three sensor coordinates during the passage of tracked vortices. Vortices were identified from simulated velocity fields using the Q-criterion \cite{hunt1988,kolar2007},
\begin{equation}
Q=\frac{1}{2}\left(\lVert \Omega \rVert^2-\lVert S \rVert^2\right),
\end{equation}
where $\Omega$ and $S$ are the antisymmetric (rotation) and symmetric (strain) components of the velocity-gradient tensor. Positive $Q$ identifies regions where rotation dominates strain. Each detected vortex was assigned to its nearest sensor and retained only if its core was within 32 simulation-grid cells (32 mm) of that sensor. This sensor-proximity threshold was selected heuristically by visual inspection to exclude remote structures and was not intended to represent a calibrated biological sensing distance. LilyPad uses a three-grid-cell numerical bounding region around the body. To avoid extracting vortices directly at that boundary, the baseline minimum body distance was doubled to six grid cells (6 mm). Sensitivity to thresholds of 3 and 12 mm was also evaluated.

For each sensor, a simulated pressure segment was defined as a contiguous interval of frames during which at least one retained vortex assigned to that sensor was visible. Simulated pressure values at the sensor coordinate were extracted over that interval and labelled by the generating model, fish-shaped or cylinder. The corresponding experimental sensor signal over the same time interval formed a measured pressure segment. This terminology distinguishes the 493 video samples from the multiple temporal pressure segments that could be extracted from a sample.

\subsection{Comparative pressure-signal classification}

To compare variable-duration pressure segments in a common feature space, simulated segments were mean-centred, amplitude-normalised and resampled to a common length. Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) then provided compact pressure-mode bases retaining 75\%, 95\% or 99\% of simulated variance \cite{klema1980}. These three thresholds tested whether conclusions depended on the amount of pressure variation retained. A separate linear classifier was constructed for every combination of simulation variant and retained-variance level. Each classifier distinguished pressure segments generated by the fish-shaped and cylinder simulations. The deliberately simple architecture provided a task-specific, data-driven comparison score rather than a conventional distance defined a priori. Related studies have similarly learned representations or similarity and discrepancy functions from data, although using different architectures and objectives \cite{hadsell2006,pacchiardi2021,tkachev2021}.

Measured pressure segments underwent the same preprocessing and were projected into the corresponding simulated pressure-mode space. A measured segment classified as fish-like was therefore more consistent with the fish-shaped model than with the cylinder in that feature space, not a direct reconstruction of the experimental wake. Outputs below confidence thresholds ranging from 0.50 to 0.99 were assigned to an undetermined class.

Several simulated pressure segments could originate from one source video. These segments inherited the same measured trajectory and, for fish simulations, the same contour sequence. A random segment split could therefore place intervals derived from the same recording in both fitting and evaluation data, making apparent fish--cylinder separation partly dependent on source-video characteristics. A secondary grouped robustness check tested whether separation persisted when entire source videos were excluded from centroid fitting. For the x-deformation variant, 189 fish and 189 cylinder segments from 27 source videos were projected into the existing 99\% pressure-mode basis. Seven complete videos were held out in each of 100 repetitions. Fish and cylinder centroids were calculated from the remaining videos, and every held-out segment was assigned to its nearest centroid. This check did not retrain the primary classifier. Because the precomputed SVD basis included all eligible segments, it tested grouped robustness at the centroid-classification stage rather than providing fully nested grouped validation.

Figure \ref{fig:classifier} summarises the main classification workflow. Training settings, dataset sizes and performance are reported in supplementary sections S3--S5. Reproduction files are available at \cite{lagneaux2026}.

\subsection{Dependence among pressure segments from the same fish}

Multiple measured pressure segments could originate from one video and therefore from the same fish. If within-video segments were strongly clustered, treating all segments as equivalent observations could allow a small number of fish to dominate segment-level patterns. This dependence was assessed in a measured-pressure SVD space using variance-weighted one-way intraclass correlation coefficients and same-video versus different-video distances. Details are provided in supplementary section S2.

\subsection{Interpreting vortex features}

The next objective was to determine which physical vortex properties underlay fish-like and cylinder-like pressure classifications. Pressure-based labels were propagated to the vortices associated with each segment. A second linear classifier used interpretable descriptors including duration, displacement, path length, distance to the body and sensor, speed, radius, area, Q-criterion magnitude and mean core position. SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values estimated the contribution of each descriptor to classification \cite{lundberg2017}. Further details are provided in supplementary section S8.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Dependence among segments from the same fish}
Potential clustering by video identity was quantified to determine whether pressure segments originating from the same fish could dominate segment-level patterns. In a measured-pressure SVD space retaining 99.1\% of variance, the variance-weighted ICC(1) was 0.058, indicating that video identity accounted for approximately 6\% of retained measured-pressure variance. The median same-video distance was 7.006 and the median different-video distance was 7.443, giving a distance ratio of 0.941. Fish identity therefore contributed little to the observed pressure variation, although segments originating from the same source video should not be interpreted as independent biological replicates. Full results are reported in supplementary section S2.

\subsection{Classifier performance on simulated pressure segments}
Before applying the comparative metric to experimental measurements, the distinguishability of pressure segments generated by the fish-shaped and cylinder simulations was tested. Test accuracies ranged from 0.55 to 0.84 across model variants and retained-variance levels. The x-deformation model achieved 0.84 at both 75\% and 95\% retained variance. The simulated classes were therefore separable but overlapping. These moderate accuracies support comparative use of the classifier but do not justify treating its output as direct wake reconstruction (supplementary table S4). In the secondary grouped check, the nearest-centroid rule retained a mean fish--cylinder accuracy of 0.747 across 100 repetitions when complete source videos were held out. Although this check used a different classification rule, it showed that source separation persisted when the fitting and evaluation data did not share video identities.

\subsection{Consistency of measured pressure signals with the models}
Figure \ref{fig:result95SVD} shows, for each model variant, the fractions of measured pressure segments classified as fish-like, cylinder-like or undetermined as the confidence threshold increased from 0.50 to 0.99, with 95\% of simulated pressure variance retained.

Across most variants and confidence thresholds, measured pressure segments were classified more often as fish-like than as cylinder-like. This suggests that the hydrodynamic signature of a swimming fish depended on more than its path through the water. A cylinder following the same trajectory did not reproduce the same pressure features, whereas the deforming fish-shaped model produced signals more consistent with the experimental measurements. Although the digital twin was not a complete reconstruction of the wake, the results indicate that body profile and deformation leave detectable signatures in the pressure field.

These proportions should be interpreted alongside the simulated test accuracies of 0.55--0.84. The classifier provided a comparative measure of consistency between two overlapping model classes, particularly for variants with lower simulated test accuracy, rather than a direct probability that an experimental wake was generated by either model.

The strongest separation occurred when both $x$ and $y$ boundary-motion components were included without the size constraint. With the 95\% retained-variance basis and a 0.99 confidence threshold, 132 of 436 measured segments were fish-like (30.3\%, 95\% Wilson binomial CI 26.2--34.7\% \cite{wilson1927}) and 19 were cylinder-like (4.4\%, 95\% Wilson binomial CI 2.8--6.7\%). The remainder were undetermined. The difference between fish-like and cylinder-like fractions remained approximately constant over the evaluated confidence thresholds (Figure \ref{fig:result95SVD}).

To test whether this pattern depended on the dimensionality-reduction threshold, the analysis was repeated with bases retaining 75\% and 99\% of simulated variance. Both produced the same qualitative pattern (supplementary figures 1 and 2), indicating that the main comparison was not specific to the 95\% basis.

\subsection{Effects of boundary-motion forcing and size constraint}

Adding the $x$ component of body-boundary motion while retaining the same image-derived contour sequence produced the clearest increase in consistency with measured pressure. The x-deformation variant produced the largest fish-like fraction and the smallest cylinder-like fraction at the strictest threshold. This comparison tested whether local in-plane motion beyond the conventional combination of $x$-directed progression and $y$-dominated undulation affected the pressure signature.

In contrast, imposing the first-frame profile did not improve agreement with measured pressure. The size-constrained variant reduced separation between fish-shaped and cylinder pressure segments, and measured classifications varied more with confidence threshold. Combining the profile constraint with two-component forcing also failed to improve agreement over the unconstrained x-deformation variant.

\subsection{Vortex descriptors: persistence and geometry}

The vortex-feature analysis focused on the x-deformation model with 95\% retained variance and a 0.99 confidence threshold. SHAP analysis indicated that the most influential descriptors were vortex displacement (the length of the straight line between the initial and final positions of the vortex), mean distance to the generating body, mean core x-position, path length (the total distance travelled by the vortex) and duration. Fish-like classification was therefore associated primarily with where vortices travelled, how far they moved, how long they persisted and how their trajectories developed, rather than with vortex size or intensity alone. 

Figure \ref{fig:shap} shows these effects for the analysed subset. Cylinder-like vortices, represented by positive classifier contributions, tended to have shorter durations and smaller mean positions. Their combinations of maximum and mean radius and speed were consistent with stronger initial structures that decayed more rapidly, while their displacement relative to path length indicated straighter trajectories. Fish-like vortices, represented by negative contributions, tended to persist longer and follow more curved, spatially distributed paths. These are associations within the selected subset rather than direct measurements of temporal decay rate.

To determine whether the result depended on excluding vortices close to the generating body, the minimum body distance was varied from 3 to 12 grid cells (3--12 mm; Figure \ref{fig:distance_filter}). Fish-like fractions generally increased when near-body structures were excluded more strongly. In the x-deformation subset, the baseline 6 mm filter gave 30.3\% fish-like and 4.4\% cylinder-like segments at the 0.99 threshold. Reducing the filter to 3 mm produced 24.8\% fish-like and 10.6\% cylinder-like classifications. Increasing it to 12 mm produced 37.9\% and 7.2\%, respectively.

\section{Discussion}
\subsection{Fine-scale motion and model realism}

The simulated classifiers first established that sparse pressure signals contained enough source-related information to separate the fish-shaped and cylinder models, although test accuracy varied substantially among model variants. This agrees with previous demonstrations that local flow measurements can classify canonical wakes or identify idealised obstacles \cite{colvert2018,lakkam2019}. The present results extend that principle to pressure signals associated with measured motion from freely swimming fish. Separation also persisted in the secondary check when complete source videos were held out, although that check used a simpler nearest-centroid rule. The moderate test accuracies and overlap between simulated classes remain important. Classification of measured pressure represents comparative consistency with two models rather than a reconstruction of the experimental wake or a probability that either model exactly generated the signal.

Measured wall-pressure segments were more consistent with a deforming fish-shaped digital twin than with a cylinder following the same centre-of-mass path. Path matching alone therefore did not preserve all source-related pressure features. Controlled virtual-swimmer comparisons similarly show that body form and kinematics have distinct effects on swimming performance and smaller-scale wake structure \cite{borazjani2010}. Pressure and vorticity scaling also differ between anguilliform and carangiform swimmers, linking surrounding flow fields to body form and swimming mode \cite{daghooghi2025}. The present result complements studies in which cylinders, foils, articulated swimmers and robotic fish isolate particular hydrodynamic mechanisms \cite{jiao2021,verma2018,feng2024,cui2024}. Anatomically resolved simulations further show that hydrodynamic performance can depend on realistic kinematics even when trajectory and swimming conditions remain controlled \cite{khalid2021,zhou2025}. These models remain valuable for controlled questions, but the present comparison shows that their level of geometric and kinematic constraint matters when the target observable is pressure measured from a freely swimming fish.

The original $y$-only boundary forcing was a deliberate first approximation. Fish locomotion is commonly represented as progression along the swimming direction combined with a lateral body wave whose amplitude increases towards the tail \cite{sfakiotakis1999,li2021}. This approximation appeared reasonable because most trajectories followed the tank's $x$ axis and most visible undulation occurred along $y$. The stronger agreement obtained with two-component forcing shows that the simplification was more consequential than expected. This supports the interpretation that apparent longitudinal redistribution of the projected body surface, local extension and compression of the projected contour, tail-tip motion towards or away from the head and other secondary in-plane movements contributed to pressure signatures reaching the sensors. Here, longitudinal and lateral refer approximately to the predominant trajectory only. The implemented components remained fixed tank-axis coordinates rather than instantaneous fish-centred axes.

\subsection{Size regularisation and foil-like surrogates}

The poorer agreement of the size-constrained model suggests that apparent frame-to-frame contour variability was not purely segmentation noise. Because a three-dimensional fish was reconstructed from one camera, projected contour changes may also reflect posture, roll or depth-dependent shape. Applying a fixed first-frame profile can therefore remove real projected variation together with detection error.

The constrained model can be viewed as an intermediate, foil-like representation. It retained a fish-derived profile and bending spine but imposed a fixed characteristic shape, placing it conceptually between the rigid circle and the less-constrained image-derived fish. This is an analogy rather than a third classification class. It echoes a broader limitation of flexible foils and robotic fish. Even with realistic profiles or controlled actuation \cite{maertens2016,li2021,feng2024,cui2024}, regularising body size and shape may suppress posture-dependent variations that contribute to a living fish's pressure signature.

\subsection{Vortex persistence and spatial organisation}

Individual-vortex analysis associated fish-like pressure classifications more strongly with trajectory geometry, spatial organisation and persistence than with raw intensity. The distance-filter sensitivity adds context to this pattern. Reducing the exclusion distance admitted more near-body structures and increased the cylinder-like fraction, whereas stronger exclusion generally increased the fish-like fraction. One possible explanation is that the cylinder reproduced some immediate, short-lived disturbances associated with body passage. Structures that persisted and travelled farther from the body were more consistent with the deforming fish model. The change was not strictly monotonic for every class and model, so distance should be treated as a practical proxy rather than a direct measurement of vortex persistence.

This distinction is biologically relevant because a following fish encounters the wake after the generating fish has moved on. Persistent vortices can provide a longer-lived hydrodynamic signal than instantaneous near-body pressure fluctuations. This interpretation is compatible with work on schooling and vortex exploitation \cite{liao2003}, but whether conspecifics use the specific pressure differences identified here remains an open behavioural question.

Fine-scale deformation may vary with swimming style, behavioural state, acceleration, manoeuvring, body roll or morphology, and could consequently alter downstream pressure signatures. This raises the possibility that wakes contain information about individual identity or intent. Neither possibility was tested or demonstrated here. Direct behavioural observations of followers combined with pressure measurements would be required to determine whether fish detect or use such variation.

\subsection{Limitations and future directions}

The video reconstruction and simulations were two-dimensional approximations of a three-dimensional swimming animal. Samples where fish were not fully submerged, or where interaction with the free surface and out-of-plane motion made the two-dimensional approximation unsuitable, were excluded from the simulation analysis. Body roll, depth changes, free-surface interactions and spanwise wake structure may all affect the real pressure field. A second camera view would be required to reconstruct the three-dimensional body motion.

Three wall-mounted pressure sensors provided sparse indirect measurements. They were non-invasive but could not resolve full wake geometry. The 32 mm sensor-proximity threshold used to associate simulated vortices with sensors was a visually selected processing heuristic rather than a calibrated biological sensing distance. Future experiments should use denser pressure measurements, including sensors away from the wall, to test whether downstream fish use the detected pressure signatures in following, schooling or predator–prey contexts.

Distance from the body was used as a practical proxy for wake persistence. Future work should involve tracking of vortex persistence through time relative to behaviourally relevant follower sensing.

Fish/video identity explained a small fraction of measured-pressure SVD variance. Segments originating from the same source video should nevertheless not be interpreted as independent biological replicates. Residual individual or recording effects may contribute to segment-level pressure signatures and should be modelled explicitly in larger datasets.

\section{Conclusion}

This study shows that the biological value of a fish-shaped digital twin lies not only in geometric complexity, but in
preserving fine-scale body motion that can affect pressure and vortical signatures in fish wakes in ways that
are detectable from sparse wall-pressure measurements. The deforming fish-shaped model was more consistent with measured pressure segments than a rigid cylinder following the same path, especially when boundary motion along both tank axes was included. Analysis of individual vortices linked this difference to persistent, spatially organised structures rather than raw intensity alone. Simplified surrogates may therefore omit biologically meaningful source-related hydrodynamic information that remains detectable downstream. The same comparative approach could inform bioinspired underwater sensing and the modelling of other complex swimming bodies, as well as control strategies for underwater robotics.

The next step is to combine three-dimensional reconstruction, richer sensing and data assimilation between measurements and simulations to allow the digital twin to move beyond model comparison and towards direct inference of the hydrodynamic cues available to fish during following and predator-prey interactions.
