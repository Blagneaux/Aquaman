\begin{abstract}
Swimming fish generate hydrodynamic signatures that may be detected by other animals. A key goal, therefore, is to measure and model these signatures accurately. However, it remains unclear how faithfully simulations must represent fish movement and body deformation to reproduce the pressure signals generated as fish swim. Here, we test whether a deforming, fish-shaped digital twin reproduces pressure measurements from swimming striped eel catfish (\textit{Plotosus lineatus}) more closely than a simple cylinder following the same motion paths. Moreover, we assess how changes to the representation of the fish's motion and posture affect model performance. Measured pressure was more consistent with the deforming fish model, especially when boundary motion along both tank axes was communicated to the flow solver. For conservative parameters, this model classified $30.3\%$ of measured segments as fish-like and $4.4\%$ as cylinder-like. Pressure-conditioned vortex analysis showed that cylinder-associated vortices were larger, had higher mean core $Q$, and occupied a greater fraction of their shorter pressure segments, while fish-associated vortices were more numerous and followed straighter trajectories. These results show the value of measuring and retaining fine-scale body deformation in digital twin models of fish motion.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Swimming animals generate hydrodynamic wakes characterised by vortices, velocity fluctuations and pressure variations. These wakes vary with an animal's body size and swimming movements \cite{drucker2000,videler2002,hanke2004} and retain traces of its recent movements \cite{weihs1975}, so they can carry information about the animal that produced them. Fish detect local hydrodynamic disturbances through the lateral-line system, which responds to water motion and pressure gradients associated with nearby flow structures \cite{coombs1988,franosch2009,chagnaud2007}. They can also exploit vortices to reduce the energetic cost of swimming, for example behind obstacles or alongside other fish \cite{liao2003,li2021}. Hydrodynamic wakes therefore provide a potentially rich source of sensory information about the presence and movements of other animals.

Given the potentially rich information contained in hydrodynamic wakes, a key goal is to measure and reproduce the flows generated by swimming fish. This is challenging because experimental measurements around freely swimming animals typically capture only part of the surrounding flow, for example one or a few planes illuminated for particle image velocimetry \cite{tytell2004,hanke2004}, while attempts to constrain or instrument the animal may alter its behaviour. Physical models, such as mechanically driven foils and robotic fish, offer a complementary approach by allowing body motion and flow conditions to be controlled and repeated. For example, robotic platforms allow researchers to manipulate gait, tailbeat phase and body-wave motion \cite{li2021}, and robotic flappers that generate fish-like wakes have been used to study how live fish interact with controlled vortices \cite{thandiackal2023}. Similarly, stationary cylinders placed in a flow produce repeatable vortex streets and have been widely used to study how fish respond to vortices \cite{liao2003jeb,liao2007}. These physical models help isolate particular mechanisms of wake production and sensing, but their prescribed movements and constrained deformation represent only a subset of the motions exhibited by living fish.

A second challenge is determining how faithfully a model must represent a fish's morphology and movements to reproduce its wake. Computational models span a wide range of complexity, from finite-dipole representations that abstract body shape and swimming kinematics \cite{gazzola2015} to articulated swimmers with only a few degrees of freedom \cite{jiao2021} and anatomically detailed simulations with deforming bodies \cite{borazjani2010,daghooghi2025,khalid2021}. Comparisons of virtual swimmers with different combinations of body form and swimming kinematics show that both affect swimming performance, and that body shape alters the smaller-scale features of the wake even though its large-scale structure depends mainly on the Strouhal number \cite{borazjani2010}. Similarly, simulations of lamprey and mackerel show that the pressure around the body scales differently in the two swimming modes \cite{daghooghi2025}, while jack-fish simulations show that measured kinematics can produce different hydrodynamic performance from prescribed motions \cite{khalid2021}. These findings establish the importance of morphology and kinematics, but do not resolve which features are necessary to reproduce the wake of a freely swimming fish. In particular, it remains unclear when reproducing the animal's trajectory is sufficient and when local body deformation and changes in body profile must also be represented.

Here, we test whether a deforming, fish-shaped digital twin reproduces the pressure signatures of swimming fish more accurately than a simulated rigid cylinder following the same observed centre-of-mass trajectories. We further test whether this comparison depends on how the fish's body contour is measured and modelled, specifically whether regularising contour size and representing local boundary motion along both tank axes improve agreement with experimental recordings. Pressure measurements from three wall-mounted sensors provided a sparse, non-invasive reference, while a classifier trained exclusively on simulated fish and cylinder pressure signals assessed which model produced signatures more consistent with these measurements. By holding trajectory constant while varying the representation of body shape and motion, we evaluate whether incorporating measured body deformation improves the model's ability to capture experimental pressure signatures. Finally, we characterise the simulated vortices associated with measured pressure signals that were confidently attributed to each model, to identify which flow features distinguish the two.

\section{Materials and methods}

\subsection{Experimental data and sample accounting}

Striped eel catfish (\textit{Plotosus lineatus}, $n = 40$) were recorded individually in 40 trials, each lasting 5 min. Fish swam freely in a tank measuring 104 $\times$ 22 cm, with a water depth of 20 cm, and could move into and out of the camera's field of view. Three wall-mounted pressure sensors were used in this study: two facing each other on opposite long walls, 41 cm from the right-hand end of the tank, and a third on one of these walls, 62 cm from the right-hand end. Infrared video was recorded at 100 frames s$^{-1}$ with a resolution of 2000 $\times$ 1200 pixels. Fish body contours and sensor locations were visible within the same camera view, allowing fish position and body motion to be related to pressure measurements at the tank wall.

The 40 videos were divided into 493 discrete samples in which a fish was visible, and body contours were extracted using the HAACHAMA pipeline \cite{lagneaux2024,lagneaux2025} (Figure \ref{fig:haachama}). Following quality assessment, 403 samples (81.7\%) were retained for analysis. These comprised 367 samples processed without manual intervention, 17 that required substantial temporal cropping, 6 with localised segmentation inaccuracies and 13 with a transient error in which the extracted contour became circular. The remaining 90 samples were excluded. All filtering was completed before simulations and classifier analyses. Full sample counts and exclusion categories are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

\subsection{Digital twins and simulation variants}

To reconstruct each fish's movement, we represented its body outline in each retained video frame as a closed contour. The resulting contour sequences captured both displacement and changes in body shape through time and were used to drive a deforming, fish-shaped digital twin in LilyPad \cite{maertens2015}. To test whether these geometric and deformational details influenced the simulated flow, we also modelled a rigid cylinder with a diameter based on the fish's body width, driven along the same measured centre-of-mass trajectory as the fish. This comparison held the observed path constant while varying body geometry and deformation.

We defined the simulation coordinates relative to the tank, with $x$ along its long axis and $y$ along its transverse axis. Fish swam predominantly along $x$, so these components approximately separated forward movement from lateral undulation. However, the axes remained fixed to the tank rather than rotating with the fish, meaning that their correspondence to forward and lateral motion changed as the animal turned.

We used this framework to investigate two sources of uncertainty in reconstructing the fish's shape and motion. First, contours extracted independently from successive frames varied in projected size. These fluctuations could reflect segmentation noise, changes in posture or out-of-plane movements such as body roll. To assess whether constraining this variation improved agreement with measured pressure, we fitted a body profile derived from the first frame along the reconstructed spine in every subsequent frame. This preserved body bending while regularising projected size, allowing us to test how frame-to-frame variation in the extracted profile affected the simulated hydrodynamic signature.

Second, we examined how the representation of local body motion influenced the simulated flow. In LilyPad, a moving body acts on the surrounding water through the velocity prescribed at its boundary: fluid in contact with the body surface is driven to move with that surface \cite{maertens2015}. We refer to this coupling as boundary forcing. In the original implementation, each contour point was placed at its measured $x$ and $y$ position in every frame, but the boundary velocity passed to the flow solver was derived only from the transverse ($y$) motion of the body. This is consistent with a common approximation for idealised undulatory swimming, in which the body progresses along the swimming direction while deformation is dominated by lateral undulation \cite{sfakiotakis1999}. In the recorded sequences, however, fish did not always move parallel to $x$, and body deformation could itself include an $x$ component---for example, as the fish turned, or as the tip of the tail moved towards or away from the head. Under $y$-only forcing, such motion changed the position of the boundary but was not transmitted to the fluid. We therefore modified the forcing so that the velocity of each boundary point was calculated directly from its displacement between consecutive time steps, in both $x$ and $y$, with local averaging over neighbouring boundary points. Comparing the two implementations allowed us to test whether including both components of boundary motion improved agreement with measured pressure.

Combining these two comparisons produced four simulation variants (Figure \ref{fig:models}). The Raw variant used the original image-derived contours and $y$-only boundary forcing. The Size-constrained variant used the first-frame body profile fitted along the reconstructed spine, while retaining $y$-only forcing. The X-deformation variant retained the original contours but included both $x$ and $y$ components of boundary motion. Finally, the Full variant combined size regularisation with two-component boundary forcing, allowing us to assess their separate and combined effects on the simulated pressure signatures. Because the cylinder geometry was already fixed, the size constraint did not change it. Further implementation details are provided in Supplementary Sections S6 and S7.

\subsection{Flow simulations}

With the digital twins and their variants defined, we next simulated the flow that each body would generate in the experimental tank. For every retained video sample, we simulated the fish-shaped digital twin under each of the four variants, together with the corresponding cylinder driven along the same trajectory. These simulations were performed in two dimensions using LilyPad \cite{maertens2015}, a fast computational fluid dynamics solver. To keep the simulated conditions as close as possible to those of the experiments, we reproduced the tank as seen by the camera, modelling its two long walls as solid boundaries, starting with the water at rest and placing the three pressure sensors at the positions observed in the video. Each simulation then advanced one video frame at a time, so that simulated and measured pressure shared a common timeline. The resulting flow fields allowed us to identify the vortices generated by each body and to extract the pressure it produced at each sensor.

\subsection{Simulated vortices and pressure segments}
\label{sec:vortices}

We first identified vortices in the simulated velocity fields using the Q-criterion \cite{hunt1988,kolar2007},
\begin{equation}
Q=\frac{1}{2}\left(\lVert \Omega \rVert^2-\lVert S \rVert^2\right),
\end{equation}
where $\Omega$ and $S$ are the antisymmetric (rotation) and symmetric (strain) components of the velocity-gradient tensor, respectively. Positive $Q$ identifies regions where rotation dominates strain. To focus on structures near the pressure sensors, we assigned each detected vortex to its nearest sensor and retained it only when its core lay within 32 simulation-grid cells of that sensor. This proximity threshold was chosen heuristically through visual inspection to exclude remote structures. It was not intended to represent a calibrated biological sensing distance. We also excluded vortices within six grid cells of the simulated body, twice the width of LilyPad's three-grid-cell numerical bounding region, to avoid detections directly at this boundary. Results obtained with alternative exclusion distances of 3 and 12 grid cells are reported in Supplementary Section S10. Vortices detected in successive frames were linked into tracks when their cores were separated by no more than three grid cells, so that each track followed a single vortex through time (Supplementary Section S8).

We then used the passage of these simulated vortices to define the intervals over which simulated and experimental pressure signals were compared. For each sensor, we identified contiguous intervals during which at least one retained vortex assigned to that sensor was present. Simulated pressure was extracted at the sensor's coordinates throughout each interval, producing a pressure segment labelled according to the generating model: fish-shaped or cylinder. The same interval was then used to extract the corresponding pressure segment from the experimental recording at that sensor. Thus, simulated vortex passages determined when pressure was sampled from the real recordings. Vortices were not detected directly in the experimental data, which comprised pressure measurements at three wall locations rather than a spatially resolved flow field. A single video sample could yield multiple pressure segments, distinguishing these intervals from the 493 video samples described above.

\subsection{Comparative pressure-signal classification}

To compare pressure segments of different durations and amplitudes, we first subtracted the mean from each simulated segment, normalised its amplitude and resampled it to a common length. This allowed the comparison to focus on the shape of pressure fluctuations rather than differences in baseline pressure, overall amplitude or duration. We then used singular value decomposition (SVD) to represent each segment as a combination of a smaller number of characteristic patterns of pressure variation \cite{klema1980}. We retained enough patterns to account for 75\%, 95\% or 99\% of the variance of the simulated signals, allowing us to assess whether our conclusions depended on retaining finer details of those signals. For each combination of simulation variant and retained-variance threshold, we trained a separate linear classifier to distinguish pressure segments generated by the fish-shaped model from those generated by the cylinder. This deliberately simple approach provided a comparison score learned from the simulated signals, rather than relying on a predefined measure of similarity. Related studies have also learned signal representations or measures of similarity and discrepancy from data, although with different architectures and objectives \cite{hadsell2006,pacchiardi2021,tkachev2021}.

We then applied the same preprocessing to the measured pressure segments and represented them using the pressure patterns learned from the corresponding simulations. This allowed each experimental segment to be assessed by the trained classifier: a segment classified as ``fish-like'' was more consistent with the simulated fish-shaped signals than with the cylinder signals. This classification therefore measured relative agreement with the two models, rather than reconstructing the experimental wake. To assess how the results depended on classification confidence, we applied thresholds ranging from 0.50 to 0.99, assigning segments that fell below the selected threshold to an ``undetermined'' category.

Because a single video, and therefore a single fish, could contribute several measured pressure segments, we also assessed whether segments from the same fish were strongly clustered, which would allow a few individuals to dominate segment-level patterns. Using intraclass correlation coefficients and comparisons of same-video and different-video distances in a measured-pressure SVD space, we found that video identity accounted for approximately 6\% of the retained measured-pressure variance (Supplementary Section S2). Fish identity therefore contributed little to pressure variation, although segments originating from the same video should not be interpreted as independent biological replicates.

\subsection{Distributional comparison of pressure-conditioned vortices}

Having assessed whether measured pressure segments were more consistent with the fish-shaped or cylinder simulations, we next examined the physical properties of the simulated vortices associated with those classifications. We focused on the X-deformation variant with 95\% of simulated pressure variance retained because it achieved the highest test accuracy on simulated pressure segments and produced the clearest and most threshold-stable separation between fish-like and cylinder-like classifications of the measured segments (Section \ref{sec:results}). We restricted this analysis to classifications meeting the 0.99 confidence threshold. Measured segments classified as fish-like were linked to vortices in the corresponding fish-shaped simulation, while cylinder-like segments were linked to vortices in the corresponding cylinder simulation. Undetermined segments were excluded. The comparison therefore concerned subsets of simulated vortices selected through their associated measured-pressure classifications, rather than all vortices produced by either model.

To characterise these vortices, we retained tracks with at least 10 consecutive valid, visible detections within the associated pressure segment. We quantified vortex size using mean and maximum area and equivalent-circle radius---the radius of a circle with the same area as the vortex. Movement was described by displacement, total path length, straightness (displacement divided by path length), and mean and maximum speed. We also measured persistence as the number of detected frames and as a proportion of the pressure segment's duration. Mean and maximum core Q-criterion magnitude characterised the dominance of rotation over strain, while mean distances to the body and sensor, together with mean $x$ and $y$ core coordinates, described where each vortex occurred.

We compared these properties at three levels to account for differences in the number of vortices and pressure segments contributed by each recording. First, we summarised descriptor distributions across all retained associations between vortex tracks and pressure segments. A physical track overlapping more than one sensor-specific segment contributed to each relevant association, so these associations did not necessarily represent distinct vortices. Second, we averaged each descriptor within each pressure segment and gave the resulting segment summaries equal weight, preventing segments containing many vortices from dominating the comparison. We also characterised vortex abundance within each segment using the number of tracks, total vortex-frame occupancy (the sum of visible detections across tracks), and the mean and maximum number of vortices visible simultaneously.

Finally, we averaged the segment summaries within each source video separately for the fish-like and cylinder-like groups. For videos represented in both groups, we calculated paired cylinder-minus-fish differences, allowing comparisons within the same recording. We reported sample counts, medians and interquartile ranges at each level. For the paired-video comparisons, in which each video contributed one difference, we additionally reported mean differences and 95\% confidence intervals calculated using Student's $t$-distribution. Further details are provided in Supplementary Section S8.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Classifier performance on simulated pressure segments}
Before using the classifiers to assess the experimental recordings, we tested whether they could distinguish pressure segments generated by the fish-shaped and cylinder simulations. Test accuracy ranged from 55\% to 84\%, depending on the simulation variant and the proportion of pressure variance retained (Supplementary Section S5, Table S4). The X-deformation variant, which retained the measured body contours and included boundary motion along both tank axes, achieved 84\% accuracy with either 75\% or 95\% of simulated variance retained. Thus, the two simulated bodies produced distinguishable pressure patterns, although their signals overlapped sufficiently to prevent perfect classification. These results support using the classifiers to assess whether experimental pressure segments are more consistent with one model than the other, while recognising uncertainty in individual classifications.

\subsection{Consistency of measured pressure signals with the models}
Having established that the classifiers could distinguish the simulated pressure signals, we applied them to the experimental recordings to assess whether measured pressure patterns were more consistent with the deforming fish-shaped model or the cylinder following the same trajectory. Comparing the four simulation variants allowed us to examine whether this relative consistency changed when projected body size was regularised, when boundary motion along both tank axes was included, or when both modifications were combined. Figure \ref{fig:result95SVD} shows the proportions of measured pressure segments classified as fish-like, cylinder-like or undetermined for each variant, with 95\% of simulated pressure variance retained. We increased the confidence threshold from 0.50 to 0.99 to assess whether classifications persisted under progressively stricter criteria, assigning segments below each threshold to the undetermined category.

Across most simulation variants and confidence thresholds, measured pressure segments were classified more frequently as fish-like than as cylinder-like (Figure \ref{fig:result95SVD}). Because both simulated bodies followed the same observed centre-of-mass trajectories, this pattern suggests that trajectory alone was insufficient to account for the measured pressure signatures. Representing the fish's body profile and deformation generally produced pressure patterns more consistent with the experimental recordings.

The clearest preference for the fish-shaped model occurred in the X-deformation variant, which retained the original measured contours and included boundary motion along both tank axes. With 95\% of simulated pressure variance retained and a confidence threshold of 0.99, 132 of 436 measured segments were classified as fish-like (30.3\%; 95\% Wilson binomial CI: 26.2--34.7\% \cite{wilson1927}), compared with 19 classified as cylinder-like (4.4\%; 95\% CI: 2.8--6.7\%). The remaining 285 segments (65.4\%) were undetermined. Although increasing the confidence threshold reduced the proportion of segments assigned to either model, the difference between the fish-like and cylinder-like proportions remained approximately constant. Thus, the relative preference for the fish-shaped model persisted under increasingly stringent classification criteria, even though most segments could not be assigned confidently at the highest threshold. This result supports including local body motion along both tank axes when modelling the pressure signatures of freely swimming fish.

Constraining projected body size did not produce a corresponding improvement. Fitting the first-frame body profile along the reconstructed spine reduced the separation between simulated fish-shaped and cylinder pressure signals, and the experimental classifications became more sensitive to the confidence threshold. Combining this size constraint with two-component boundary forcing also failed to improve agreement relative to the X-deformation variant. Retaining frame-to-frame variation in the measured contours therefore provided a more useful representation for this comparison, although these results do not establish whether that variation arose from genuine changes in posture, out-of-plane motion or segmentation error.

The main pattern was also robust to the amount of simulated pressure variance retained during dimensionality reduction. Repeating the analysis with bases retaining 75\% and 99\% of simulated variance produced the same qualitative results (Supplementary Figures 3 and 4), indicating that the preference for the fish-shaped model was not specific to the 95\% representation. Likewise, at the 0.99 threshold, more segments were classified as fish-like than as cylinder-like in every variant when the minimum vortex-to-body distance was reduced to 3 or increased to 12 grid cells (Supplementary Section S10).

These findings should nevertheless be interpreted in light of simulated test accuracies ranging from 55\% to 84\%. The classifiers distinguished overlapping model classes and therefore provided only a relative measure of consistency, particularly for variants that performed less well on simulated test data. Their confidence scores therefore do not give the probability that either model correctly represents an experimental wake. Rather, the results show that incorporating body shape and deformation can improve agreement with measured pressure patterns beyond that achieved by reproducing trajectory alone.

\subsection{Fish-associated vortices were smaller, straighter and more numerous}

The pressure-conditioned subset contained 574 associations between fish-model vortex tracks and pressure segments, representing 551 distinct tracks from 88 measured pressure segments and 21 source videos, and 65 cylinder-model associations, representing 65 distinct tracks from 15 segments and 10 source videos. Here, a track denotes a single simulated vortex followed through successive frames (Section \ref{sec:vortices}). Ten source videos were represented in both groups. We present the paired comparison within these ten videos as the main result because it compares the two models within the same recordings (Figure \ref{fig:vortex_distributions}).

Within these videos, cylinder-associated vortices were larger than fish-associated vortices, with mean paired differences (cylinder minus fish) of +8.18 grid cells in mean area (95\% $t$-based CI +2.54 to +13.83) and +0.490 grid cells in mean equivalent-circle radius (+0.15 to +0.83). They also had higher mean core $Q$ (+0.0101; +0.0011 to +0.0190) and spanned a greater fraction of their pressure segment (+0.147; +0.020 to +0.274). In contrast, fish-associated vortices followed straighter trajectories (straightness difference $-0.0652$; $-0.116$ to $-0.014$) and were more numerous: cylinder-associated segments contained 2.42 fewer tracks ($-4.24$ to $-0.59$) and 329.5 fewer vortex frames ($-534.1$ to $-124.9$). Cylinder-associated pressure segments were also 76.9 frames shorter ($-128.6$ to $-25.2$), whereas absolute vortex duration did not differ clearly ($-13.2$ frames; $-43.1$ to $+16.7$). Cylinder-associated vortices therefore persisted for a similar absolute time within shorter pressure events. Differences in displacement, path length, maximum speed, distance from the body, core position and the number of simultaneously visible vortices varied among videos, and their intervals included zero.

These intervals are not adjusted for the number of descriptors examined, and with ten paired videos the supporting evidence is the consistent direction of the differences rather than any single interval. The same directions were obtained when all vortex-track associations were pooled and when each pressure segment was given equal weight (Supplementary Section S8, Supplementary Tables S5--S7 and Supplementary Figures 1 and 2). These are distributional associations and do not establish that any individual vortex caused the classification of its pressure segment.

\section{Discussion}
\subsection{Fine-scale motion and model realism}

The simulated classifiers first established that sparse pressure signals contained enough source-related information to separate the fish-shaped and cylinder models, although test accuracy varied substantially among model variants. This agrees with previous demonstrations that local flow measurements can classify canonical wakes or identify idealised obstacles \cite{colvert2018,lakkam2019}. The present results extend that principle to pressure signals associated with measured motion from freely swimming fish. The moderate test accuracies and overlap between simulated classes remain important. Classification of measured pressure represents comparative consistency with two models rather than a reconstruction of the experimental wake or a probability that either model exactly generated the signal. In addition, because the classifiers were trained and tested with a segment-level split, their performance on entirely unseen fish was not evaluated (Supplementary Sections S2 and S5).

Measured wall-pressure segments were more consistent with a deforming fish-shaped digital twin than with a cylinder following the same centre-of-mass path. Path matching alone therefore did not preserve all source-related pressure features. Controlled virtual-swimmer comparisons similarly show that body form and kinematics have distinct effects on swimming performance, and that body form in particular alters the smaller-scale wake structure \cite{borazjani2010}. Pressure scaling also differs between anguilliform and carangiform swimmers, linking the surrounding pressure field to swimming mode \cite{daghooghi2025}. The present result complements simulation studies in which articulated swimmers, self-propelled swimmers and model robotic fish isolate particular hydrodynamic mechanisms \cite{jiao2021,verma2018,feng2024,cui2024}. Anatomically resolved simulations of jack fish further show that hydrodynamic performance can depend on realistic kinematics even when trajectory and swimming conditions remain controlled \cite{khalid2021}. These models remain valuable for controlled questions, but the present comparison shows that their level of geometric and kinematic constraint matters when the target observable is pressure measured from a freely swimming fish.

The original $y$-only boundary forcing was a deliberate first approximation. Fish locomotion is commonly represented as progression along the swimming direction combined with a lateral body wave whose amplitude increases towards the tail \cite{sfakiotakis1999}. This approximation appeared reasonable because most trajectories followed the tank's $x$ axis and most visible undulation occurred along $y$. The stronger agreement obtained with two-component forcing shows that the simplification was more consequential than expected. This supports the interpretation that apparent longitudinal redistribution of the projected body surface, local extension and compression of the projected contour, tail-tip motion towards or away from the head and other secondary in-plane movements contributed to pressure signatures reaching the sensors. Here, longitudinal and lateral refer approximately to the predominant trajectory only. The implemented components remained fixed tank-axis coordinates rather than instantaneous fish-centred axes.

\subsection{Size regularisation and foil-like surrogates}

The poorer agreement of the size-constrained model suggests that apparent frame-to-frame contour variability was not purely segmentation noise. Because a three-dimensional fish was reconstructed from one camera, projected contour changes may also reflect posture, roll or depth-dependent shape. Applying a fixed first-frame profile can therefore remove real projected variation together with detection error. More generally, both the video reconstruction and the simulations were two-dimensional approximations of a three-dimensional animal. Body roll, depth changes, free-surface interactions and spanwise wake structure may all affect the real pressure field, and a second camera view would be required to reconstruct three-dimensional body motion.

The constrained model can be viewed as an intermediate, foil-like representation. It retained a fish-derived profile and bending spine but imposed a fixed characteristic shape, placing it conceptually between the rigid circle and the less-constrained image-derived fish. This is an analogy rather than a third classification class. It echoes a broader limitation of flexible foils and robotic fish, whether physical or simulated. Even with realistic profiles or controlled actuation \cite{maertens2017,li2021,feng2024,cui2024}, regularising body size and shape may suppress posture-dependent variations that contribute to a living fish's pressure signature.

\subsection{Vortex properties of fish and cylinder wakes}

Among the vortices associated with confident classifications, cylinder-associated vortices were larger, had higher mean core $Q$ and occupied a greater fraction of their pressure segment, whereas fish-associated vortices were more numerous and followed straighter trajectories. To our knowledge, the vortices generated by a swimming fish and by a rigid body following the same trajectory have not previously been compared directly, but these contrasts are consistent with established differences between bluff-body and fish wakes. A cylinder moving relative to the water typically sheds a drag-type K\'arm\'an vortex street, whose vortex scale and shedding frequency are set primarily by its diameter and the Strouhal number \cite{williamson1996,williamson1998}. Swimming fish, by contrast, generate wakes shaped by the travelling body wave. In a continuously swimming mullet, for example, vortex spacing matched tail-beat amplitude and stride length \cite{muller1997}, and was therefore set by the swimming kinematics rather than by a single body dimension. Fish and flapping foils that produce thrust also shed reverse K\'arm\'an streets, in which vortex orientation is reversed relative to the drag wake \cite{triantafyllou1991,thandiackal2023}. In anguilliform swimmers such as eels, the wake consists of lateral jets separated by shear layers that roll up into two or more vortices in each half tail-beat \cite{tytell2004,tytell2004b}. The elongated, eel-like body of \textit{P. lineatus} may therefore distribute vorticity among more numerous and smaller structures than a cylinder of similar width, consistent with the fish-associated vortices observed here. This analysis was nevertheless conditioned on confident outputs of the pressure classifier and does not describe every vortex generated by either model.

Cylinder-associated pressure segments were also roughly half as long as fish-associated segments, while the vortices within them persisted for a similar absolute time and therefore spanned a greater fraction of the event. Combined with their larger area and greater mean core $Q$, these distributions raise the hypothesis that the cylinder can produce a fish-consistent pressure pattern through fewer, larger, higher-$Q$ vortices concentrated in a brief event, while the fish-shaped model may distribute its pressure contribution among multiple smaller, lower-$Q$ structures succeeding one another over a longer one. The analysis did not resolve the contribution of individual vortices to pressure or test pressure superposition. Because segment boundaries were set by the continued visibility of retained vortices, the detection and retention criteria influence segment length as well as the counts and durations measured within it, and tracking fragmentation could contribute further.

The sensitivity of the classifications to the minimum vortex-to-body distance adds context to these distributions (Supplementary Section S10). Reducing the exclusion distance admitted more near-body structures and increased the cylinder-like pressure fraction, whereas stronger exclusion generally increased the fish-like fraction. One possible explanation is that the cylinder reproduced some immediate disturbances associated with body passage, but fewer of the structures that persisted further from the body. The change was not strictly monotonic for every class and model, so distance remains a practical spatial filter rather than a calibrated measure of vortex persistence.

This distinction is biologically relevant because a following animal encounters a wake after the generating fish has moved on. Fish wakes can retain a clear vortex structure for at least 30 s and remain measurable for several minutes \cite{hanke2000,hanke2004}. Piscivorous catfish and harbour seals can track hydrodynamic trails left by other animals \cite{pohlmann2001,pohlmann2004,schultepelkum2007}, and seals can also follow artificial trails generated by a miniature submarine \cite{dehnhardt2001}. The structure of a trail also matters. Harbour seals can discriminate wakes generated by objects of different size and shape, and flow measurements suggest that vortex size, the spatial extent of the wake and the arrangement of vortices may provide the relevant cues \cite{wieskotten2011}. Trails generated by a gliding rather than an actively propelled body are also smaller, decay faster and are lost more often by a tracking seal \cite{wieskotten2010}. Differences in vortex size, number and arrangement such as those identified here could therefore, in principle, inform a follower about the source of a wake. Much of our understanding of how fish respond to vortices nonetheless comes from wakes behind stationary cylinders \cite{liao2003jeb,liao2007,chagnaud2007}, whereas the wake behind a moving, oscillating body can be more irregular and more challenging for a downstream fish \cite{ortegajimenez2021}. Our comparison, in which a cylinder followed the trajectory of a real fish, suggests that rigid-body surrogates may omit features of the wakes generated by freely swimming fish.

This limitation is also relevant to engineered systems designed to generate controlled wakes. Because the wake of a single fixed cylinder is dominated by one length scale, set by its diameter, several approaches have used moving or multiple cylinders to produce more varied flows. A single cylinder oscillating transversely in a uniform stream can already shed two single vortices, two vortex pairs, or a pair and a single vortex per cycle, depending on its oscillation amplitude and frequency \cite{williamson1988}. When two cylinders oscillate in still water, a gap jet strengthens vortex shedding when they are placed close together side by side, whereas in tandem, vortices of the same sign merge and elongate into a symmetric pattern \cite{li2022}. Arrays of fixed cylinders have also been designed computationally to produce either periodic or less periodic vortex streets in which fish swimming can be studied \cite{sparks2024}, and small rotating cylinders placed just behind a larger one can reshape the flow around it under control strategies learned directly from experiments \cite{fan2020}. Our results indicate which features such systems would need to reproduce to emulate a freely swimming fish rather than a bluff body. Within the pressure-conditioned subset, a single cylinder following the fish's path was associated with fewer, larger vortices with higher core $Q$ and less straight trajectories, concentrated in briefer pressure events. These vortex descriptors could therefore serve as quantitative targets when designing or optimising multi-cylinder or robotic wake generators intended to reproduce biologically realistic hydrodynamic signals.

Fine-scale deformation may vary with swimming style, behavioural state, acceleration, manoeuvring, body roll or morphology, and could consequently alter downstream pressure signatures. This raises the possibility that wakes contain information about individual identity or intent. Neither possibility was tested or demonstrated here, and whether conspecifics detect or use the specific pressure differences identified here remains an open behavioural question. Direct behavioural observations of followers combined with pressure measurements would be required to determine whether fish detect or use such variation.

\section{Conclusion}

This study shows that the biological value of a fish-shaped digital twin lies not only in geometric complexity, but in
preserving fine-scale body motion that can affect pressure and vortical signatures in fish wakes in ways that are detectable from sparse wall-pressure measurements. The deforming fish-shaped model was more consistent with measured pressure segments than a rigid cylinder following the same path, especially when boundary motion along both tank axes was included. Pressure-conditioned vortex distributions linked this difference to contrasts in vortex size, number, mean core $Q$, relative vortex duration and trajectory straightness. Simplified surrogates may therefore omit biologically meaningful source-related hydrodynamic information that remains detectable downstream. The same comparative approach could inform bioinspired underwater sensing and the modelling of other complex swimming bodies, as well as control strategies for underwater robotics.

The next step is to combine three-dimensional reconstruction, richer sensing and data assimilation between measurements and simulations to allow the digital twin to move beyond model comparison and towards direct inference of the hydrodynamic cues available to fish during following and predator-prey interactions.
