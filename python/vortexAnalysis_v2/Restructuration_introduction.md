Paragraph 1: Hydrodynamic wakes are likely to contain information	

* Swimming animals generate hydrodynamic wakes composed of vortices and pressure fluctuations.	
* These wakes are likely to contain information about the generator, including body size, swimming kinematics, trajectory, tail-beat frequency and recent movement history.	
* Many fishes detect these hydrodynamic disturbances using the lateral line, which measures local pressure gradients and water motion.	
* Introduce evidence that fishes exploit wake information for behaviours such as schooling, predator/prey interactions, rheotaxis and potentially wake following.	
* End by emphasising that wakes are therefore potentially information-rich sensory cues.



Paragraph 2: Hydrodynamic wakes are generated by different sources

* Hydrodynamic wakes are generated by many different sources, including swimming animals, passive objects (e.g. rocks and cylinders) and engineered systems (e.g. robotic fish and underwater vehicles).
* Fish detect these wakes using the lateral line, but they only have access to sparse local pressure information rather than the complete flow field.
* This raises a fundamental biological question: does sparse pressure information contain enough information to infer what generated the wake, allowing fish to distinguish other animals from non-living objects?
* The same question has technological importance, as solving this inverse problem could enable passive detection and classification of underwater objects using sparse pressure sensors.
* Whether sparse pressure measurements alone contain sufficient information to distinguish between different wake generators remains unknown.

Paragraph 4: Which aspects of swimming encode hydrodynamic information?

* Many studies represent swimming fish using simplified models, such as cylinders, foils, dipoles or robotic swimmers, because they are computationally efficient and reproduce many large-scale properties of fish wakes.
* However, it is unclear which aspects of a fish's morphology and movement generate the pressure signatures available to the lateral line. A cylinder following the same trajectory may reproduce broad wake characteristics, but body shape, lateral body undulations and tail-beat kinematics could generate additional pressure features that are absent from simplified models.
* Consequently, we do not know what level of biological realism is required to preserve the hydrodynamic information contained in a fish's wake, or which aspects of swimming are most important for encoding that information.
* Determining which pressure signatures are preserved or lost by simplified models is therefore essential for understanding hydrodynamic sensing in fishes.
